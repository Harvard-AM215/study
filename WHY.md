# Why the study skill works the way it does

Each choice in the study skill is listed below with the research behind it: what the studies
found, how strong the evidence is, and where it stops. Where a choice rests on course policy, on
practical judgment, or on stretching a result beyond what was tested, this page says so.

No study has tested this particular skill. The evidence comes from other students, other
subjects and other tools, and part of the reason for publishing this page is to find out what
we have missed or got wrong. If you know of research that supports, contradicts or refines any
of these choices, please tell us: [the last section](#suggesting-research) explains how.

Every paper is linked by its DOI, a permanent identifier that opens the publisher's page; the
Harvard Library gives you access to most of these journals. Where a free copy exists, there is a
second link to it.

## Summary

| What the skill does | Main evidence | How strong (our reading) |
|---|---|---|
| Asks you questions before explaining anything | Practice testing (retrieval practice) | Strong for remembering studied material; weaker for new kinds of problems |
| Asks you to try even when you do not know yet | Unsuccessful attempts; problem solving before instruction | Moderate; guidance can work better for beginners |
| Does not rely on how clear an explanation feels | Feeling of learning versus actual learning | Moderate |
| Gives one hint, then another try, then an explanation | Tutoring research | Indirect; the exact rule is our judgment |
| Uses these rules because it is an AI | Studies of learning with AI, with and without safeguards | Recent and mixed; design matters |
| Uses the textbook as the source of truth | Language models produce false statements | The problem is well documented; that our method reduces it is untested |
| Looks for where your understanding stops | Matching instruction to prior knowledge; accuracy of self-judgment | Indirect; the exact method is our judgment |
| Tells you whether each answer was right | Feedback after testing | Moderate; feedback can also backfire |
| Mixes different kinds of questions | Interleaved practice | Moderate for mixing problem types; mixing question formats is our extrapolation |
| Asks you "why" | Self-explanation | Moderate |
| Has you lay out a chapter's main ideas from memory, then check them | Retrieval practice, including concept mapping done from memory | Moderate; what helps is recalling, not the map format |
| Brings you back to the same map each week | Spaced practice | Strong for spacing; weekly reuse of the map is untested |
| Ends with a plan of specific sections and exercises | Which study techniques work | Strong that practice beats rereading; the plan's format is our judgment |

## 1. Asking before explaining

**What the skill does.** Every session is mostly questions that you answer on your own. The
skill explains only after you have tried.

**Why.** For material you need to remember later, trying to recall it usually strengthens it
more than reading it again. This is called **retrieval practice**, or the **testing effect**.

- Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students study prose passages and then either take recall
  tests or restudy the passages. On a test five minutes later, restudying was ahead. On tests
  two days and one week later, the students who had been tested remembered substantially more,
  even though restudying had made the other students more confident that they would remember.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)
- Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan (2017) combined the results of many studies (a
  **meta-analysis**) and found practice tests more beneficial for learning than restudying and
  every other comparison condition. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)
- Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) reviewed ten common study techniques. Practice testing was one
  of only two rated "high utility", because its benefits hold for learners of different ages and
  abilities and on many kinds of tests. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)

**Limits.** A quiz asks new questions, not the ones you practised. Pan and Rickard (2018)
looked at whether practice tests help with *different* questions (**transfer**). On average
they do (d = 0.40), but after correcting for publication bias, transfer often disappeared unless
the practice asked for elaborated answers, required the same kind of response as the final
question, or was answered well in the first place. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151)
This is one reason the skill asks "why" questions and not only for results.

## 2. Trying before you know the answer

**What the skill does.** It asks the question even when you have not studied the topic, and it
treats "I don't know, but I think…" as a useful answer.

**Why.** An attempt that fails can still prepare you to learn the answer.

- Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) compared attempting to answer a question before being shown
  the answer with reading the question and the answer together. The attempts helped later
  memory even when they failed. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729)
- Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009) asked students questions about an essay before they read it.
  They did better on a later test than students who spent the time reading, even on the
  questions they had got wrong at first. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496)
- Sinha and Kapur (2021) combined 53 studies comparing problem solving *before* instruction with
  instruction first. Problem solving first came out ahead (g = 0.36), and more so when the design
  followed the principles of "productive failure" closely.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105) ·
  [free PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/00346543211019105)

**Limits.** The first study used made-up general-knowledge questions and word associations,
and the second an essay about vision. In the third,
instruction first was better for young children (grades 2–5) and for general skills. And for
beginners, a large body of research finds that studying worked examples and guided instruction
work better than being left to solve problems unguided. Sweller and Cooper (1985) found that
algebra students who studied worked examples spent less time and then solved similar problems
faster than students who solved problems from the start.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3) Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) review this
evidence and argue that minimal guidance is less effective for learners without enough prior
knowledge. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1) This is why the skill
does not leave you stuck: it gives a hint after a wrong answer and explains after your second
try, and it uses the textbook's worked examples by asking you to predict the result before you
look.

## 3. How clear an explanation feels, and how much you learn

**What the skill does.** It checks what you can do instead of asking whether an explanation made
sense.

**Why.** Feeling that you learned something and having learned it are different, and they can
point in opposite directions.

- Deslauriers and colleagues (2019), in introductory physics at Harvard, randomly assigned
  students to active-learning classes or to lectures by highly rated instructors covering the
  same content. The students in active classes learned more, but felt they had learned less: at
  first they took the extra effort as a sign of learning poorly, though the authors note that
  students can come to see its value over a semester.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116) ·
  [free PDF](https://www.pnas.org/content/pnas/116/39/19251.full.pdf)
- Carpenter, Wilford, Kornell and Mullaney (2013) showed students a video of an instructor
  explaining a concept either fluently or haltingly. The fluent version made students predict
  they would remember more, but it did not change how much they learned.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0442-z) ·
  [free PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2Fs13423-013-0442-z.pdf)
- In Roediger and Karpicke (2006), section 1, restudying raised students' confidence while
  producing worse retention a week later.

## 4. One hint, then another try, then an explanation

**What the skill does.** After a wrong or incomplete answer it gives one small hint and lets you
try again. It explains only after that second attempt.

**Why.** Tutoring can work without the tutor doing the explaining.

- Chi and colleagues (2001) stopped human tutors from giving explanations and feedback and
  encouraged them to prompt the student instead. The students learned just as effectively.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2504_1)
- VanLehn (2011) reviewed studies comparing tutoring with no tutoring. Human tutoring had an
  average effect of d = 0.79, and intelligent tutoring systems, most of which work through a
  problem step by step with the student, reached nearly the same (d = 0.76).
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369)

**Limits.** No study we know of tested "one hint, then one more try, then an explanation". How
much help to give, and when, is an open question in the research, which Koedinger and Aleven
(2007) call the **assistance dilemma**. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0) The rule
is our judgment.

## 5. Why an AI tutor needs these rules

**What the skill does.** The skill exists so that your agent behaves like a tutor that makes you
do the work, not like an answer machine.

**Why.** Studies of learning with AI (trials of AI tutors, a laboratory writing experiment and
observations of programmers) find outcomes that depend partly on how the tool is designed and
used.

- Bastani and colleagues (2025) gave nearly 1,000 high-school mathematics students access to
  GPT-4 during practice. With a standard chat interface, practice scores rose by 48%, but when
  access was taken away the students did 17% worse than students who never had it: they had used
  it as a crutch. A version that was given the correct solutions and teachers' notes on common
  mistakes, and told to give hints without stating answers, raised practice scores by 127% and
  largely avoided the harm. The unrestricted version gave a wrong answer about half the time when
  asked for one. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
- Fan and colleagues (2025) compared 117 university students writing an essay in a laboratory
  study, with ChatGPT, with a human expert, with an analytics tool, or alone. The ChatGPT group
  improved their essays most, but their knowledge gain and transfer did not differ significantly
  from the other groups, and the authors warn of "metacognitive laziness".
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544) ·
  [free PDF](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1111/bjet.13544)
- Prather and colleagues (2024), in 21 laboratory sessions, observed and interviewed novice
  programmers using generative AI. Students who struggled often finished believing they had done
  better than they had.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3632620.3671116)
- Kestin and colleagues (2025), in physics at Harvard, found that students learned more in less
  time from an AI tutor built on the same teaching principles as the course's active-learning
  classes than from those classes. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6) ·
  [free PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6.pdf)
- Liffiton and colleagues (2023) built CodeHelp, an AI tutor for a programming course designed
  not to reveal solutions, and deployed it to 52 students, who valued it.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3631802.3631830) ·
  [free PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3631802.3631830)

**Limits.** These are different students, subjects and tools from ours, the designs of the
helpful tutors bundled several features together, and most of the studies are recent. None
tested this skill.

## 6. The textbook as the source of truth

**What the skill does.** It reads the published chapters that `scripts/get_textbook.py` downloads
from the textbook's public site, refreshed at the start of a session when the agent can reach
the internet, so it reads the same text you do. It opens the textbook section
before asking or judging, names it, and records "not assessed" when the textbook does not settle
whether your answer is right. It prefers the textbook's own exercises, whose solutions are
printed, over questions it invents.

**Why.** Language models can produce fluent text that is unsupported or wrong, which the
research calls **hallucination**; Ji and colleagues (2023) survey the problem and attempts to
reduce it.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3571730) In Bastani and colleagues' study (section 5), the
tutor that helped was given the correct solutions, and the unrestricted one was often wrong.

**Limits.** Grounding the agent in the textbook is meant to reduce its errors, but no study has
tested whether this particular way of doing it does, and it will not remove them. That is why
the form asks whether the agent told you something the textbook does not support, and why you
should check the textbook when something looks wrong.

## 7. Finding where your understanding stops

**What the skill does.** It makes questions harder after right answers and narrows in after
wrong ones, until it has found one thing you can do and one thing you cannot for each concept.

**Why.** Instruction works best when it matches what you already know, and students are not
reliable judges of that.

- Kalyuga (2007) reviews the **expertise reversal effect**: techniques that help learners with
  little background can stop helping, or get in the way, for learners with more.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3) ·
  [free copy](http://handle.unsw.edu.au/1959.4/50601)
- Dunlosky and Rawson (2012) found that students who judged their own answers more accurately
  remembered more two days later, and this was not explained by how many practice rounds they
  took. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.08.003)
- Kornell and Bjork (2007) review how students decide what to study and for how long: good
  decisions depend on accurate monitoring of what you know.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194055) ·
  [free PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03194055.pdf)

**Limits.** The specific method, one right answer and one wrong answer per concept, is our
judgment, adapted from a personal learning system by Amos Blomqvist
([learn](https://github.com/amosblomqvist/learn)). It has not been tested as such.

## 8. Feedback on every answer

**What the skill does.** After every answer, including right ones, it says whether you were
right and points to the textbook section.

**Why.** Butler, Karpicke and Roediger (2008) gave students a multiple-choice test on general
knowledge, with feedback on half of the questions. Feedback improved later recall, and it
doubled retention of answers that were right but given with low confidence.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.34.4.918)

**Limits.** Feedback is not always helpful. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) combined 607 results: on
average feedback improved performance (d = 0.41), but more than a third of the feedback
interventions made performance worse, and feedback helped less the more it drew attention away
from the task and toward the person. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) The
skill's feedback is therefore about the answer and the textbook section, never about you. The
evidence does not show that feedback on every answer is best.

## 9. Mixing different kinds of questions

**What the skill does.** It mixes calculations, "why does this step work", "what if this
assumption failed" and "which of these ideas applies".

**Why.** Rohrer and Taylor (2007) had college students practise several kinds of mathematics
problems either grouped by kind or mixed. A week later, the students who practised mixed
problems did far better. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8) Brunmair and Richter
(2019) combined many studies of mixing (**interleaving**): the overall effect was moderate
(g = 0.42) and smaller for mathematics (g = 0.34), and grouping was better for learning lists of
words. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)

**Limits.** These studies mixed kinds of problems or categories. Mixing kinds of *questions*
(calculation, explanation, what-if) is our extrapolation.

## 10. Asking you to explain why

**What the skill does.** It asks you to explain why steps hold, and when it explains, it starts
from something you already know and shows why the next step is needed.

**Why.** Explaining to yourself why each step holds (**self-explanation**) improves
understanding. Chi and colleagues (1994) found that students prompted to explain each line of a
text to themselves learned more, and the students who explained most built correct mental
models. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3) Bisra and colleagues (2018) combined 64
research reports on prompting self-explanation: g = 0.55.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x) Dunlosky and colleagues (2013, section 1) rated
self-explanation "moderate utility".

**Limits.** This research is about students generating explanations. How the agent itself
explains is our extrapolation.

## 11. Laying out the main ideas from memory

**What the skill does.** Each session starts with you writing down a chapter's main ideas and how
they connect, without looking. You then check that map against the chapter and repair what was
missing or wrong, and the map is kept in `my/map.md` with each idea marked recalled, added or
repaired.

**Why.** Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found that retrieval practice produced more learning from
science texts than building concept maps with the text in view, even on a final test that asked
students to create concept maps. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327) Blunt and
Karpicke (2014) then had students build concept maps from memory: that and writing a paragraph
from memory both beat restudying a week later, and the map did no better than the paragraph.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035934) O'Day and Karpicke (2021) found that building a concept
map before retrieval practice added nothing to retrieval practice alone.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000486) In these studies the benefit tracked retrieving from
memory, not whether the answer was written as a paragraph or as a map. The skill uses a map because the question it asks
is about structure, meaning which idea depends on which, and a map makes that structure visible
to check. Constructing a map yourself has also been associated with more learning than studying
a map someone else made (Schroeder and colleagues, 2018, g = 0.72 against 0.43, each against its
own comparison). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9)

**Limits.** These studies used short science texts, not chapters with derivations; they gave
students repeated rounds of recall, where a session here has one; and none used an agent. Applying them to this course's chapters, and checking the map with the agent rather
than by rereading, is our inference.

## 12. Coming back to the same map each week

**What the skill does.** It picks up where your last session left off, and the plan spreads the
work over the week.

**Why.** Studying in several spaced sessions beats one long session. Cepeda and colleagues
(2006) combined 317 experiments on remembering verbal material and found that the best gap
between sessions grows with how long you need to remember.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) ·
[free PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3rr6q10c/qt3rr6q10c.pdf?t=lnr1h0) Dunlosky and
colleagues (2013, section 1) rated spaced (**distributed**) practice "high utility".

**Limits.** Coming back each week to the same map has not been tested as such.

## 13. A plan made of specific sections and exercises

**What the skill does.** The plan lists textbook sections, worked examples to redo and exercises
to try, not "review chapter 6".

**Why.** Dunlosky and colleagues (2013, section 1) rated rereading and highlighting, the
techniques students use most, as low utility, and recommend practice testing in place of
rereading. A plan made of exercises keeps your next study session active.

**Limits.** The format of the plan is our judgment; the evidence is only that practice beats
rereading.

## 14. How the multiple-choice questions are written

**What the skill does.** Wrong options are built from real mistakes and look like the right
one, so that the answer cannot be guessed from its wording.

**Why.** Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez (2002) validated a set of 31 guidelines for writing
multiple-choice questions against 27 textbooks and 27 research studies and reviews. The skill's rules for wrong
options follow the kind of guidance collected there; the specific rules are ours.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15324818AME1503_5)

## Choices that rest on policy or practical judgment

- **Public material only.** Course policy: non-public course material is not given to AI tools.
- **No promises about what is assessed.** The syllabus says quizzes "may draw on anything
  covered in lectures, readings, weekly exercises, and past P-Sets".
- **Nothing personal.** Privacy: the skill does not need your name or grades to help you study.
- **About 30 minutes by default, and one cycle per session.** One chapter mapped from memory,
  checked, and one link repaired, with more practice only if you want it. A practical length
  that leaves time to come back later in the week (section 12). No study we know of tested it.
- **Checking against the headings and the "Connections" section first.** A quick way to see
  what the chapter treats as its main ideas before rereading any paragraph in full. Our judgment.
- **Repairing one link in your own words, with one question on it.** Restating a corrected idea
  is a form of self-explanation (section 10), and one question checks whether you can use the link
  straight away; the choice of
  one link is our judgment.
- **Recalled / added / repaired, and secure / partial / not yet / not assessed.** Plain labels
  for your own use, not researched instruments.

## Suggesting research

If you know a study that bears on any of these choices, or one that argues against them, we
would like to hear about it. Open an issue on this repository with the **Research suggestion**
template, or propose an edit to this page in a pull request (see `CONTRIBUTING.md`). Please
include:

1. The citation and its DOI or a permanent link.
2. What the study did and found: who took part, what was compared, and the result.
3. Which choice above it supports, contradicts or refines, and what you would change.

We add a paper after reading at least its abstract, prefer peer-reviewed work, and mark
preprints as such. Contributing is optional and is not graded; issues and pull requests are
public and show your GitHub account.
